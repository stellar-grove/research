Predators, prey and the paradox of enrichment

Feed the prey more and you'd expect a steadier, larger system. Why does it start to oscillate instead?

Chapter 10 · Further dynamical systems. A worked example rebuilt from the chapter's topic in Mike Mesterton-Gibbons, *A Concrete Approach to Mathematical Modelling*; the scenario and numbers are our own illustration, not the book's text.

Headline: Below a carrying capacity of 3.0 the populations settle down; above it the equilibrium turns into an unstable spiral and the system locks onto a limit cycle. At K = 5 prey swing between 0.0027 and 3.56 every 25 time units.

  • 3.0carrying capacity at which steady coexistence is lost (Hopf point)
  • 0.0027prey at the bottom of each cycle at K = 5 (peak 3.6)
  • 25period of the cycle (time units)

The problem

Prey grow logistically; predators eat them but can only handle so many at once (a saturating, Holling type II functional response). Enriching the prey's environment (raising K) sounds good for both species. What actually happens?

The model (Rosenzweig–MacArthur)

dxdt=rx(1−xK)−axy1+ahx,dydt=eaxy1+ahx−my

Predators hold steady when their intake balances deaths, which fixes the prey level independent of K:

x*=ma(e−mh),y*=ra(1−x*K)(1+ahx*)

The Jacobian at this point has trace zero, and its eigenvalues cross the imaginary axis (a Hopf bifurcation), when x* sits at the hump of the prey nullcline. Stability requires

K<KHopf=2x*+1ah

With r=a=1, h=0.5, e=0.5, m=0.2: x*=0.50 and KHopf=3.00. At K=2.5 the equilibrium is a stable spiral; at K=5 it is an unstable spiral surrounded by a stable limit cycle (its trace is +0.080).

Key findings

  • Enrichment destabilises: raising K past 3.0 does not raise the steady prey level (it is pinned at 0.50 by the predators) but triggers cycles that grow with K.
  • Big swings bring real risk: at K = 5 prey fall to 0.0027 and predators to 0.2 at the troughs; in a real population with chance events (Chapter 5) such lows invite extinction.
  • A limit cycle is robust, unlike the neutral cycles of the classic Lotka–Volterra model (which conserve dynamics.lotka_volterra_invariant): disturb it and it returns to the same loop.
from books.concrete_mathematics import dynamics as dy
print(dy.rm_equilibrium(K=5.0, r=1, a=1, h=0.5, e=0.5, m=0.2))
t, z = dy.settle(dy.rm_rhs, [1.0, 0.5], (1, 5.0, 1, 0.5, 0.5, 0.2), 2000, 200)
print([round(v, 2) for v in dy.cycle_stats(t, z[0])])     # prey min, max, period

# → RMEquilibrium(x=0.5, y=1.125, exists=True, stable=False, K_hopf=3.0)
# → [0.0, 3.56, 24.78]

Assumptions and extensions

  • The paradox is model-dependent. Prey refuges, inedible prey or predator interference stabilise the system; in the lab (rotifers and algae) enrichment does produce cycles.
  • Two species only. Adding a third (a top predator or a second prey) can turn the cycle chaotic.
  • The Hopf condition here was derived by hand; equilibrium.jacobian + classify check any model numerically.
