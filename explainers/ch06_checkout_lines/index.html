How many checkouts to open

Customers arrive at 1.8 a minute and take 2 minutes each to serve. How many lanes keep the wait reasonable?

Chapter 6 · Stationary distributions. A worked example rebuilt from the chapter's topic in Mike Mesterton-Gibbons, *A Concrete Approach to Mathematical Modelling*; the scenario and numbers are our own illustration, not the book's text.

Headline: The work needs 3.6 cashiers on average, but 4 lanes leave customers waiting 3.9 minutes on average. Opening one more cuts that to 0.6; meeting '80% start within 2 minutes' takes 5 lanes.

  • 3.9 minaverage wait with 4 lanes (90% busy)
  • 0.6 minaverage wait with 5 lanes
  • 5lanes for 80% of customers to start within 2 minutes

The problem

Shoppers reach the checkouts at random, λ=1.8 a minute (a Poisson process, as in Chapter 5), and service takes an exponentially distributed time with mean 2 minutes (μ=0.5). The load is a=λ/μ=3.6 busy cashiers' worth of work. With c lanes and one shared line, how long do people wait?

The model

The number of customers in the store's checkout area is a birth-death chain: arrivals at rate λ, departures at rate μmin(n,c). In the long run it settles into a stationary distribution found from the balance equations λπn−1=μmin(n,c)πn. Summing the tail gives Erlang's C formula, the probability that an arrival has to wait:

C(c,a)=acc!11−ρ∑k=0c−1akk!+acc!11−ρ,ρ=ac<1

The waiting time is zero with probability 1−C, and otherwise exponential with rate cμ−λ:

Wq=C(c,a)cμ−λ,P(wait>t)=C(c,a)e−(cμ−λ)t,Lq=λWq

Key findings

  • Waits explode near full utilisation. At 90% busy (4 lanes) the mean wait is 3.9 minutes; at 72% (5 lanes) it is 0.6, a 7-fold drop for one extra cashier.
  • Averages hide the tail. With 5 lanes, 90% of customers start within 2 minutes, but managers should staff for a percentile target, not the mean.
  • One shared line beats separate lines. Pooling lets any free cashier take the next customer; separate lines (c independent M/M/1 queues at λ/c) would give a mean wait of 18.0 minutes with 4 lanes, versus 3.9 with one line.
from books.concrete_mathematics import stationary as st
q = st.mmc(lam=1.8, mu=0.5, c=4)
print(round(q.Wq, 2), round(q.p_wait, 3), round(q.L, 2))
print(st.servers_needed(1.8, 0.5, max_wait=2, prob=0.8))

Assumptions and extensions

  • Exponential service is the M in M/M/c; real service times are less variable, which shortens waits (the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula for M/G/1 shows by how much).
  • Steady state. A lunchtime surge is a transient; at ρ near 1 the queue takes a long time to reach its stationary distribution.
  • No balking. Shoppers who see a long line leave; the finite-capacity model stationary.mmck (used in the barber explainer) handles that.
