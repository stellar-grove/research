Harvesting a fishery optimally

What stock should a sole owner keep in the sea, and how fast should it get there?

Chapter 3 · Optimal control and utility. A worked example rebuilt from the chapter's topic in Mike Mesterton-Gibbons, *A Concrete Approach to Mathematical Modelling*; the scenario and numbers are our own illustration, not the book's text.

Headline: At a 5% discount rate the owner should hold the stock at 56.8 thousand tonnes, above the maximum-yield level of 50 and far above the 20 that open access drives it to, and get there by fishing flat out for 1.1 years.

  • 56.8k toptimal stock at a 5% discount rate
  • 20k topen-access stock: fished until profit is zero
  • +2.5%present value of the optimal plan over holding the MSY stock

The problem

A sole owner (a firm or a well-run agency) harvests a logistically growing stock (r=0.5, K=100 thousand tonnes). Fish sell at a fixed price, and catching gets more expensive as the fish thin out, because more effort is needed per tonne. Future profit is discounted at rate δ=0.05. Which harvest plan maximises the present value of profit?

The model

maxh(t)∫0∞e−δt(p−cqx)hdtsubject todxdt=F(x)−h,0≤h≤hmax

The payoff is linear in the control h, so Pontryagin's maximum principle gives a bang-bang answer: fish at the maximum rate or not at all, except on a singular path where the stock is held at x*. That stock satisfies Clark's modified golden rule:

F′(x*)−c′(x*)F(x*)p−c(x*)=δ,c(x)=cqx

Read it as an investment rule: a tonne left in the sea earns its marginal growth F′, plus a stock effect (more fish make every later tonne cheaper to catch). Leave fish in until that return equals the interest rate. Without costs this reduces to F′(x*)=δ, so x*=K2(1−δ/r)=45.

With costs, x*=56.76, sustaining a catch of F(x*)=12.27 thousand tonnes a year.

Key findings

  • Discounting pulls the stock down: from 60.0 with no discounting to 56.8 at 5%. If the discount rate exceeds the stock's maximum growth rate and fishing is costless, the optimal plan is to fish it out (try δ above r with zero cost).
  • Costs push the stock up: without them the same 5% rate gives 45; a fish left in the sea makes every future fish cheaper to catch.
  • Open access is the worst case: with no owner, boats enter until profit is zero, at a stock of 20, and all the rent is lost.
  • Getting to 57 fast pays: the bang-bang plan is worth 2.5% more than running the same fleet down to the maximum-yield stock of 50.

Assumptions and extensions

  • Fixed price. If catches move the price (a downward-sloping demand), the problem becomes nonlinear and the approach is gradual rather than bang-bang.
  • A single sole owner. Two or more owners sharing the stock each ignore the others' stock effect; the outcome slides toward open access.
  • No uncertainty. Random recruitment argues for a margin above x*; Chapter 7's dynamic programming handles that.
