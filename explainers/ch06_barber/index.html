Should the barber hire an assistant?

Customers who find every chair taken walk away. Does a second barber bring in enough of them to pay a wage?

Chapter 6 · Stationary distributions. A worked example rebuilt from the chapter's topic in Mike Mesterton-Gibbons, *A Concrete Approach to Mathematical Modelling*; the scenario and numbers are our own illustration, not the book's text.

Headline: Alone, the barber turns away 38% of customers. An assistant cuts that to 9%, adding 0.90 haircuts an hour worth $22.41, against a $30 wage: don't hire (net −$7.59 an hour).

  • 38%of customers lost with one barber
  • $22.41most the assistant is worth per hour
  • 3.7/harrival rate above which hiring pays

The problem

Customers arrive at a barbershop at random, λ=3 an hour. A haircut takes on average 30 minutes (μ=2 an hour). There are 3 waiting chairs; anyone who finds them all full leaves. A haircut costs $25. Should the barber pay an assistant $30 an hour?

The model

With c barbers and room for N=c+chairs customers, the number in the shop is a birth-death chain on 0,…,N: arrivals at rate λ (blocked at N), departures at μmin(n,c). Detailed balance gives the stationary distribution:

πn=π0∏k=1nλμmin(k,c),∑n=0Nπn=1

Arrivals see the stationary distribution (Poisson arrivals see time averages), so the share turned away is πN and the shop serves λ(1−πN) customers an hour. Hiring pays if

pλ(πN(1)−πN(2))>w

Key findings

  • Lost customers are the hidden cost of being busy. One barber is busy 92% of the time and still loses 38% of arrivals, because 3 customers an hour arrive for a barber who can cut at most 2.
  • The assistant's value is the recovered customers, not the idle time saved: 0.90 extra haircuts an hour × $25 = $22.41, less than the $30 wage.
  • It turns on demand: hiring pays once arrivals exceed about 3.7 an hour (try the slider).
  • Extra chairs barely help here: two more waiting chairs with no assistant would recover 0.09 haircuts an hour ($2.24), 10% of what the assistant brings, at the cost of longer waits.
from books.concrete_mathematics import stationary as st
r = st.hire_assistant(lam=3.0, mu=2.0, chairs=3, price=25.0, wage=30.0)
print(round(r["one"].p_block, 3), round(r["two"].p_block, 3), round(r["net_gain"], 2), r["hire"])

Assumptions and extensions

  • Customers leave only when every chair is full. Real customers balk at long lines earlier; make the arrival rate fall with the number waiting (any state-dependent rates work in stationary.birth_death_stationary).
  • The assistant is as fast as the barber and is paid whether busy or not.
  • Steady demand. Saturday mornings and Tuesday afternoons deserve separate answers; part-time help is the natural compromise.
