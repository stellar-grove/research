The best speed through a tunnel

If every driver keeps a safe stopping distance, what speed gets the most cars through per hour?

Chapter 2 · Equilibrium. A worked example rebuilt from the chapter's topic in Mike Mesterton-Gibbons, *A Concrete Approach to Mathematical Modelling*; the scenario and numbers are our own illustration, not the book's text.

Headline: Throughput peaks at a surprisingly low 31 km/h (19 mph), carrying 1,491 cars an hour per lane. Greenberg's classic fit to Lincoln Tunnel data puts the best speed at 17.2 mph and capacity at 1,443 an hour: nearly the same answer, from data.

  • 31 km/hspeed that maximises flow: √(2bL), independent of reaction time
  • 1,491/hmaximum cars per hour per lane
  • 1,153/hcars per hour if everyone drives 80 km/h: faster is not better

The problem

A single-lane tunnel is the bottleneck of a commuter route. Drivers keep a gap big enough to stop if the car ahead stops dead. Faster driving moves each car along quicker, but needs bigger gaps. Which speed maximises the number of cars per hour?

The model

A driver at speed v needs a reaction distance vτ and a braking distance v2/2b, where b is the deceleration. Each car occupies its own length plus margin L plus that gap, and the flow (cars per unit time) is speed divided by spacing:

q(v)=vL+vτ+v22b

Setting dq/dv=0 gives L=v2/2b: the best speed is where the braking distance equals the car length,

v*=2bL,q*=1τ+2L/b

With L=6 m, τ=1 s and b=6 m/s², v*=8.49 m/s (30.5 km/h) and q*=0.414 cars per second.

A check against data

Greenberg (1959) fitted a speed-density law v=cln(kj/k) to Lincoln Tunnel counts, with c=17.2 mph and jam density kj=228 vehicles per mile. Its flow q=kcln(kj/k) is greatest at k=kj/e, where the speed is exactly c:

qmax=ckje

That is 1,443 vehicles an hour at 17.2 mph (28 km/h), within 3% of the safe-spacing answer, from a completely different starting point.

Key findings

  • The best speed 2bL does not depend on reaction time: alert drivers raise the peak flow (to 1,881/h at 0.5 s), but the speed limit that achieves it stays at 31 km/h.
  • At 80 km/h the lane carries only 1,153 cars an hour, 23% below the maximum: the extra gap needed to stop from speed grows with v2 and outruns the speed itself.
  • Two unrelated models, one from braking physics and one fitted to counts, agree that a tunnel lane tops out near 1,491 cars an hour at about 30 km/h; this is why tunnels set low speed limits and meter entry.

Assumptions and extensions

  • The brick-wall rule (stop even if the car ahead stops instantly) is cautious; if both cars brake equally only the reaction gap is needed, and the best speed rises.
  • Identical drivers. Mixed reaction times and vehicle lengths lower capacity; the spread matters as much as the mean.
  • Steady flow. Stop-and-go waves inside dense traffic are the subject of Chapter 11 (the traffic-light explainer).
