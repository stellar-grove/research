The yellow-light dilemma

When the light turns amber, can a driver always either stop in time or get through before red?

Chapter 9 · Building a model: adapting, extending, and combining. A worked example rebuilt from the chapter's topic in Mike Mesterton-Gibbons, *A Concrete Approach to Mathematical Modelling*; the scenario and numbers are our own illustration, not the book's text.

Headline: At 60 km/h with a 3-second amber, a driver between 25 m and 63 m from the line can do neither: a 38 m dilemma zone. Removing it takes an amber of 5.3 s at this speed.

  • 38 mdilemma zone at 60 km/h
  • 5.3 samber needed at 60 km/h
  • 5.1 samber needed at 50 km/h

The problem

A driver approaching a junction at 60 km/h sees the light turn amber. Stopping needs room; going needs time to cross a 20 m junction before red. This builds a new model by combining two earlier ones: the stopping distance (Chapters 2 and 4) and constant-speed travel.

The model

Let x be the distance to the stop line when the amber starts, δ=1 s the reaction time and b=3 m/s² a comfortable deceleration. The driver can stop if

x≥xc=vδ+v22b

and can go, holding speed, if the car's back clears the junction (width W=20 m, car length L=5 m) within the amber τ:

x≤x0=vτ−W−L

If xc>x0, drivers between the two distances can do neither: the dilemma zone (Gazis, Herman and Maradudin, 1960). It vanishes when x0≥xc, which gives the minimum amber:

τmin(v)=δ+v2b+W+Lv

At 60 km/h (v=16.7 m/s), xc=63.0 m and x0=25.0 m.

Key findings

  • The amber needed is U-shaped in speed: slow cars need long to clear the junction, fast cars need long to stop. It is smallest, 5.1 s, at 44 km/h, so a 3-second amber leaves a dilemma zone at every speed shown.
  • Set the amber for the speed limit: 5.1 s at 50 km/h, 5.3 s at 60 km/h. This is the basis of the traffic-engineering formula for amber timing (which adds the junction-clearing term as an all-red interval).
  • Combining models is mostly bookkeeping: two small, separately validated pieces answer a question neither answers alone, and the dilemma zone's existence follows from their different growth rates in v.

Assumptions and extensions

  • Constant speed through the amber. Drivers who accelerate shrink the zone (the accel argument); those who hesitate enlarge it.
  • One reaction time. Real reaction times vary from about 0.7 to 1.5 s; the zone is a band, not a line.
  • Red-light cameras and countdown timers change behaviour, which feeds back into the model's parameters.
