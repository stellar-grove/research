Which worms should a bird eat?

A big worm is worth more but takes longer to eat. When should a bird ignore small worms?

Chapter 7 · Optimal decision and reward. A worked example rebuilt from the chapter's topic in Mike Mesterton-Gibbons, *A Concrete Approach to Mathematical Modelling*; the scenario and numbers are our own illustration, not the book's text.

Headline: Rank worms by energy per second of handling: big worms (5 J/s) beat small ones (2 J/s). The bird should still eat small worms now, and ignore them once big worms are met more than 0.083 times a second, however many small worms there are.

  • 1.72 J/sbest intake rate (both kinds)
  • 0.083/sencounter rate with big worms above which small ones are ignored
  • 0effect of small-worm abundance on whether to eat them

The problem

A thrush searches a lawn. It meets big worms (e1=40 J, h1=8 s to handle) at rate λ1 per second of searching, and small ones (e2=6 J, h2=3 s) at λ2=0.5. Eating a small worm takes time that could have been spent finding a big one. Which worms should it take to maximise energy per unit time?

The model

Time alternates between searching and handling. Over a long foraging bout of search time Ts, the bird meets λiTs worms of type i; if it eats the types in its diet D, the long-run intake rate is the ratio of expected energy to expected time (Holling's disc equation, a renewal-reward ratio):

R(D)=∑i∈Dλiei1+∑i∈Dλihi

Rank types by profitability ei/hi. Adding the next type raises R exactly when its profitability beats the rate already achieved without it. For two types, small worms are worth eating when

e2h2>λ1e11+λ1h1⟺λ1<e2e1h2−e2h1

Here the threshold is λ1*=6/(40·3−6·8)=0.0833. At λ1=0.05 the specialist rate is 1.429 J/s and the generalist 1.724 J/s.

Key findings

  • The zero-one rule: a worm type is either always eaten or always ignored on encounter; there is no 'sometimes' in the optimum.
  • Whether to eat small worms doesn't depend on how many there are. With big worms common (λ₁ = 0.15), the bird ignores small worms whether they are met 0.5 or 2.5 times a second (the optimal diet is big only in both cases: confirmed).
  • Abundant good food makes a forager picky: as big worms become common the specialist's rate rises past what any small worm offers (2 J/s), so small worms drop out at λ₁ = 0.083.
  • The same renewal-reward ratio decides when to leave a depleting patch (the marginal value theorem, decision.mvt_exponential).

Assumptions and extensions

  • Sequential, random encounters and perfect recognition: the bird knows a worm's type instantly.
  • Energy rate is the currency. A bird at risk from predators may trade energy for safety, which needs the dynamic programming of the next explainer.
  • Field tests (great tits choosing mealworm pieces on a conveyor belt) found the predicted switch, with partial preferences around the threshold.
