Are poorer countries catching up?

Are poorer countries growing faster than rich ones, and how long would it take to close the gap?

Real data: World Bank World Development Indicators via the World Bank API (retrieved 2026-11-21). 183 countries with GDP per capita in both 1990 and 2024; regional and income-group aggregates excluded.

Headline: Only slowly, and only recently. Across 1990–2024 the poorest countries grew slightly faster than rich ones (slope -0.39), a half-life of about 163 years to close half the gap. Controlling for investment, population growth and schooling, convergence is much clearer: half-life ≈ 62 years.

  • 62 yearsHalf-life of the gap to each country's own steady state (conditional)
  • 163 yearsHalf-life of the gap between poor and rich countries (unconditional)
  • -0.03Change in the cross-country spread of log income, 1990–2024

Findings

  • Unconditional slope -0.39 (95% CI -0.61 to -0.18): a country with 10× lower income in 1990 grew about 0.9 points a year faster.
  • The 15-year rolling slope moved from -0.30 (windows starting 1990) to -0.33 (starting 2009), strongest at -0.69 for windows starting 2005. Catch-up is statistically clear in recent windows, but it has eased since that peak.
  • Conditional convergence is about 2.4× faster than unconditional: countries converge to their *own* steady states, set by how much they invest, how fast their populations grow and how much they school. Poor countries that invest little converge to a low level.
  • Cross-country dispersion (std. dev. of log income, 183 countries observed every year) went from 1.18 to 1.15: the spread has barely narrowed: poor countries growing a little faster hasn't yet made the world much less unequal across countries.

Method

Beta convergence: for country i, average annual growth on log starting income (conditional version adds controls):

gi=lnyi,T−lnyi,0T=α+βlnyi,0+γ1lnsi+γ2ln(ni+0.05)+γ3lnhi+εi

where si is the investment share of GDP, ni population growth (0.05 covers technology growth and depreciation) and hi secondary enrollment. The slope implies a convergence speed and half-life:

β=−1−e−λTT,half-life=ln2λ

Sigma convergence tracks σt=sdi(lnyi,t) on a balanced panel.

Caveats

  • PPP GDP for poor and conflict-affected countries is estimated with wide error bands; countries that collapsed may be missing data in later years, which flatters the picture.
  • Unweighted: each country counts once, so China and India count as much as small states. A population-weighted version would answer a different question (how fast the typical *person* is catching up).
  • Conditional convergence is about steady states, not causes: investment and schooling are themselves outcomes of development.
