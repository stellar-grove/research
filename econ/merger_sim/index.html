Merger simulation: would combining two rivals raise prices?

If two rivals merge, how much do prices rise, and would the standard screens have caught it?

Simulated market: 6 firms, 30 cities × 20 quarters, with planted demand and costs so each step can be checked against the truth. Seed 20261121.

Headline: Merging Ardent and Brio would raise their prices about 7.1% and 8.2%. The standard simulation recovers that within 0.01 points, but only with properly instrumented demand: naive OLS demand predicts 8%/9%.

  • +7.1%True price rise for Ardent after the merger
  • 1,212HHI increase (post-merger HHI 3,107)
  • 17.9%Marginal-cost savings Ardent would need to keep its price flat

Findings

  • Demand: OLS estimates price sensitivity at 0.080, well below the true 0.09, because firms charge more where demand is strong. Instrumenting with the input-cost shifter gives 0.090.
  • Understated price sensitivity makes customers look captive, so the OLS-based simulation overstates the merger's price effect by about 1.1×.
  • Rivals respond too: non-merging firms raise prices 0.37% on a share-weighted average, because the merged firm's higher prices push some customers their way.
  • Screens: ΔHHI = 1,212 to a post-merger HHI of 3,107 (presumed anticompetitive under the 2023 Merger Guidelines). GUPPI is 8.4% for Ardent and 9.5% for Brio; above ~5% usually draws scrutiny.
  • Efficiencies: the merger is price-neutral only if it cuts marginal costs by about 17.9% (Ardent) and 20.0% (Brio). Claimed synergies have to clear that bar.

Method

Logit demand: consumer n in market m values product j at unjm=δj+ξjm−αpjm+ϵnjm, giving shares and Berry’s linear estimating equation:

sjm=eδj+ξjm−αpjm1+∑keδk+ξkm−αpkm,lnsjms0m=δj−αpjm+ξjm

Prices respond to ξjm, so pjm is instrumented with the input-cost shifter wm. Firms set prices where, for each firm f with total share Sf,

pj−cj=1α(1−Sf(j))

which recovers marginal costs cj before the merger and gives the new prices after it (the merged firm’s Sf is the two products’ combined share). Screens use logit diversion and margins:

D12=s21−s1,GUPPI1=D12p2−c2p1,ΔHHI=2σ1σ2

where σj are shares among inside goods in percentage points.

Caveats

  • Plain logit forces substitution in proportion to share (independence of irrelevant alternatives). Real cases use nested or random-coefficients logit so close substitutes divert more to each other.
  • Static Bertrand ignores entry, repositioning and coordinated effects, any of which can dominate.
  • A real simulation leans on the quality of the cost shifter: a weak or invalid instrument breaks step 1.
