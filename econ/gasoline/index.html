Energy: do gas prices rise like rockets and fall like feathers?

When crude oil moves, do pump prices follow up faster than they follow down?

Real data: FRED (EIA retail gasoline and WTI spot prices), retrieved 2026-11-21. Weekly, 1991–2026 (1,870 weeks).

Headline: Only briefly. Rises reach the pump a little faster at first (84% vs 72% after two weeks), but falls catch up by week four (83% vs 96%), and the four-week difference isn't statistically significant (p = 0.43) in any of the five specifications below. In the national weekly average, rockets and feathers are at most a short lag, not a lasting gap.

  • 84%Of a crude-oil rise passed to the pump within 2 weeks
  • 72%Of a crude-oil fall passed to the pump within 2 weeks
  • 1.06¢Long-run change at the pump per 1¢/gal change in crude

Findings

  • Long run: each 1¢ per gallon change in crude moves retail gasoline about 1.06¢; the rest of the pump price (refining, distribution, taxes, margin) drifts slowly over time.
  • Asymmetry test: in the first four weeks, the response to rises and falls isn't statistically different (p = 0.43). Over just the first two weeks the gap is not significant either (p = 0.26), so even the early lead for rises could be noise.
  • Each week, the pump price closes 4% of any gap with its long-run level. Today gasoline is 6¢ above the level crude implies.
  • Studies that do find the asymmetry mostly use daily station-level prices, where local competition and search costs matter; a weekly national average smooths much of that away.

Method

Long-run relationship and asymmetric error-correction model, with gt retail gasoline and ct crude, both in cents per gallon, Δc+=max(Δc,0) and Δc−=min(Δc,0):

gt=β0+β1ct+δt+et

Caveats

  • National averages hide station-level behavior; the asymmetry is usually larger at the local level.
  • The long-run relationship drifts as taxes, refining capacity and blend requirements change.
  • WTI is one benchmark; coastal markets price off Brent and wholesale gasoline.
