Trade: who pays for tariffs?

When the U.S. puts tariffs on Chinese goods, do Chinese exporters cut their prices, or do Americans pay?

Real data: FRED (BLS import price indexes; Federal Reserve H.10), retrieved 2026-11-21. BLS import prices exclude tariffs: they measure what the foreign exporter receives.

Headline: Almost entirely U.S. buyers. Through the 2019–20 escalation, Chinese exporters' pre-tariff prices fell just 2.6% relative to other countries' (vs the months just before the tariffs), while the average tariff on Chinese goods rose about 18%. That puts 85%–100% of the tariff on the U.S. side, depending on the baseline.

  • 85%–100%Share of the 2018–19 China tariffs paid on the U.S. side
  • -2.6%Change in China's pre-tariff export prices vs other countries, 2019–20
  • -8.1%Yuan vs dollar over the same period (a weaker yuan lowers dollar prices)

Findings

  • The baseline matters: China's relative price was already drifting down 3.5% a year in 2016–18, before any tariff. Against the level just before the tariffs, the phases show -0.6%, -2.6%, -0.4%; against that pre-existing trend extended forward, +1.9%, +2.6%, +7.8%, i.e. no absorption at all.
  • Even the small decline overstates what exporters absorbed: the yuan weakened about 8% against the dollar over the same period, which by itself lowers Chinese prices in dollars.
  • 2025 round: since January 2025, China's pre-tariff prices have moved -4.6% relative to the comparison countries (+4.5% in absolute terms over the past year). The old downward drift alone would give about -6.2% over that span, so exporters again appear to be absorbing little of the much larger 2025 duties.

Method

Relative pre-tariff price of Chinese imports, against the equal-weighted comparison group C (Canada, Mexico, Japan, EU), indexed to June 2018:

Rt=100[(lnPtChina−14∑c∈ClnPtc)−(lnP0China−14∑c∈ClnP0c)]
Rt=α+∑kβkPhasek,t+εt(Jan 2018–Dec 2020),U.S. share=1−−βk/100Δτ

The trend version replaces α with a linear trend fit on January 2016–June 2018 and extended forward; shares are capped between 0 and 1.

where Δτ≈0.18 is the rise in the average U.S. tariff on Chinese imports over 2018–19 (Bown, PIIE). If exporters cut pre-tariff prices by the full tariff, −βk/100=Δτ and the U.S. share is zero.

Caveats

  • The China index covers all goods, not only tariffed ones; by late 2019 tariffs covered about two-thirds of imports from China, which biases the measured price response toward zero.
  • Trade diverted to Vietnam, Mexico and others changes which goods remain in the China index.
  • 'Paid on the U.S. side' means U.S. importers, retailers or consumers; this data can't split it among them.
