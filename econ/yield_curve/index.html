Finance: is an inverted yield curve a reliable recession signal?

When the yield curve inverts, how often does a recession follow, and what does it say now?

Real data: FRED (U.S. Treasury, NBER), retrieved 2026-11-21. Monthly averages of the daily spread, 1982–2026; NBER recession dates through October 2026.

Headline: Useful, but far from infallible. Out of sample the spread separates months that are 12 months before a recession from the rest with an AUC of 0.69, and 4 of 5 completed inversions since 1982 were followed by a recession within two years. The long 2022–2024 inversion was the exception: no recession followed. Today's spread (+0.71) implies a 9% chance of being in recession a year from now.

Findings

  • Each point of spread matters: the probit slope is -0.44, so a spread of −1 implies about 28% and +2 about 3%.
  • In real time (out of sample from 1995), months the model put at 30% or more were followed by a recession 22% of the time a year later. In-sample AUC 0.81 vs out-of-sample 0.69.
  • The real-time probabilities run too high: months the model put at 50% or more were followed by a recession 27% of the time. Read the level as a ranking of risk, not a literal probability.
  • The horizon matters: asked instead whether a recession comes *at any point* in the next 12 months, the out-of-sample AUC drops to 0.55, because inversions usually end (the curve re-steepens) before the recession arrives.
  • Lead times are long and variable: 14, 9, 17, 10 months from the start of inversion to the start of recession.
  • The 2022–24 inversion was the deepest and longest in the sample, and no recession followed. Candidate explanations: the 3-month rate was pushed up by an unusually fast hiking cycle, and the term premium on 10-year bonds had been compressed for years.

Method

With Rt the NBER recession indicator, st the 10-year minus 3-month spread and Φ the standard normal CDF, the probability of being in recession 12 months from now:

Pr(Rt+12=1)=Φ(α+βst)

Out of sample, the probability for month t uses α^,β^ fit only on months whose 12-month outcome was already known at t.

Caveats

  • Recessions are rare: five in this sample. Every statistic here rests on a handful of events.
  • NBER dates recessions months after the fact, so the most recent year can't be graded yet.
  • A single spread ignores other signals (credit spreads, the labor market) that sharpen real-time forecasts.
